\subsubsection{Illustrative examples}

As a continuation of the numerical experiment shown in the previous section, we aim to 
illustrate the effect of the data set size on the data-driven solution. To that end, we first fix the number of points $N_g$ to the value $15$ from the 1D uniaxial test and rotate the corresponding
data by $N_{\theta} = 4,~6,~8,~10$ and $20$ different angles equally distributed
over $[0,2\pi)$. 
The pressure field $\uD_h$ (contours and surface) and the flux $\sD_h$ 
solutions are shown in figure \ref{fig:sol_varNtheta}. It is also interesting
to see the distribution of the data over the domain $\Omega$. 
%We also show the dual fields $\lD_h$ and $\mD_h$ in \ref{fig:dualsol_varNtheta}.
On the other hand, in figure \ref{fig:sol_varNg} we show the solution when 
$N_{\theta} = 40$ is fixed and the number of points in the 1D uniaxial test is 
varied, namely, $N_g = 5,~10,~15,~20,~25,~30,~35$. 
The main observation is that as the total number of points in the data
set is increased the data-driven solution becomes closer to the reference solution.
Later on in this section, we show numerical evidence of this convergence for a problem
with manufactured solution.
However, this is clearly as a result of the model chosen to compute
such reference solution. For instance, if in the same case we

the reference solution and thus the data-driven one will

for the same set of values of $N_{\theta}$ of the previous experiment.
Another aspect that influenced the data driven solution is the fact that 
isotropy was assumed to compute the reference solution used
to initially distribute the data. If we consider otherwise an anisotropic behavior
we end up with different results. To that end we produce synthetic results
using Forcheimer's model with different permeability values assigned to two mutually orthogonal directions.

\subsubsection{Convergence to a manufactured solution}

We assess the convergence of the data-driven formulation and all its 
variants, namely, formulation DNLSP1-A, Aeqor, B, C and D to a manufactured solution 
proposed as the reference case. To that end we choose $ \uD \in \mathcal{C}^{\infty} $ to be s given by:
\begin{equation}
\uD:\Omega \to \mathbb{R},~p(x,y) = \cos(\pi x)\cos(\pi y), \label{eq:manup}
\end{equation}
where the domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^{2} \mid \Omega = [0,1]\times[0,1]$.
Assuming the permeability to be isotropic with $\frac{k}{\mu} = 1$ we derive
from (\ref{eq:manup})  the complete solution and corresponding source term to be
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		&\sD: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{2}, & &\sD = [-\pi\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y), -\pi \cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)]^{\top} \\
		&\eD: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{2}, & &\eD = [-\pi\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y), -\pi \cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)]^{\top} \\
		&q: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, & &q = -2\pi^{2}\cos(\pi x) \cos(\pi y),
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
First, we assess the behavior of original fomulation DNLSP1-A. We introduce a finite element 
structured mesh made of $20,000$ triangular elements and produce synthetic data 
having different values of $N_{g} \times N_{\theta}$, namely, $2 \times 2$ 
The synthetic data is produced such that the minimum and maximum values of the
pressure gradient given by the manufactured solution are captured.  

to larger configurations such as $16 \times 16$\footnote{It should be noted that, since the relationship between  $ (\sD_h^{\text{ref}},\eD_h^{\text{ref}}) $  is known \textit{a priori} based on the manufactured solution, the discrete data relating flux and pressure gradient obtained from the uniaxial test, i.e $ \mathfrak{D}_m = \left\lbrace\left(\mathfrak{g}_n,\mathfrak{s}_n\right)\right\rbrace_{n \in \mathcal{N}_m} $, should ensure that the minimum and maximum values of both fields are encompassed.}, we observed a gradual convergence of the data-driven solutions for all the unknown fields -- $\uD_h$, $\sD_h$, $\eD_h$, $\lD_h$, and $\mD_h$ -- towards the exact (or manufactured) solution.

\begin{proposition}
	For a given finite element mesh with a fixed spatial step $ h $, the data-driven solution $ (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) $ converges to the reference solution $ (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) $ in a consistent norm \( \|\cdot\| \) as the number of sampled data points $ N_{m} \rightarrow \infty$ .
\end{proposition}



\begin{proof}
	
	Let $\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}}$ be the reference functions, and let $\hat{\sD}_h$ and $\hat{\eD}_h$ be their projections onto the space $\text{DG}_0$. These projections minimize the $L^2$-norm projection errors:
	
	\[
	\| \sD_h^{\text{ref}} - \hat{\sD}_h \|_{L^2(\Omega)} \quad \text{and} \quad \| \eD_h^{\text{ref}} - \hat{\eD}_h \|_{L^2(\Omega)},
	\]
	
	implying that $\hat{\sD}_h$ and $\hat{\eD}_h$ are the closest elementwise constant functions in $\text{DG}_0$ to $\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}}$, respectively. As $N_{m} \to \infty$, the discrete data set $\mathfrak{D}_m$ becomes dense in the solution space. By definition, a set $\mathfrak{D}_m$ is dense in a solution space if every point in that space can be approximated arbitrarily closely by point from $\mathfrak{D}_m$. Specifically, for any pair $(\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h)$ in the solution space and any $\epsilon > 0$, there exist discrete data pair $\left\{\left(\mathfrak{g}_n, \mathfrak{s}_n\right)\right\}_{n \in \mathcal{N}_m}$ such that:
	
	\[
	\| (\tilde{\sD}_h|_{K_n}, \tilde{\eD}_h|_{K_n}) - (\hat{\sD}_h|_{K_n}, \hat{\eD}_h|_{K_n}) \| < \epsilon.
	\]
	
	Thus, as $N_{m}$ increases, the discrete data $\mathfrak{D}_m$ densely samples the solution space and the optimization problem ensures:
	
	\[
	\| (\tilde{\sD}_h|_{K_n}, \tilde{\eD}_h|_{K_n}) - (\hat{\sD}_h|_{K_n}, \hat{\eD}_h|_{K_n}) \| \to 0 \text{ as } N_{m} \to \infty.
	\]
	
	Thus, we proceed as follows: 
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\begin{aligned}
			\| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \| &=  \| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h)  +  (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \| \\
			&\leq \| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) \| + \| (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \|.
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation*}
	
	
	As $N_{m} \to \infty$, the term $\| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) \| \to 0$. Since $\| (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \|$ is minimized, the difference $\| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \|$ also approaches zero.
	
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Effect of noise on the input data}

So far, we have studied data-driven solutions to the Darcy problem, examining the effects of variables such as the number of finite elements in the mesh, $ (N_{x} \times N_{y}) $, the number of sampled points, $ (N_{m} = N_{g} \times N_{\theta}) $, and the convergence of the unknown data fields: the pressure field $ \uD $, the flux vector field $ \sD $, the pressure gradient field $ \eD $, and the dual fields $ \mD $ and $ \lD $ for the natural data-driven formulation and its variants. However, these studies assume a given dataset, $\mathfrak{D}_{m} = \{(\mathfrak{g}_{n}, \mathfrak{s}_{n})\}_{n \in \mathcal{N}_{m}}$, that is noise-free. The objective of this section is to investigate the characteristics of a data-driven solution when a Gaussian noise is introduced into the available data. To this end, we define the standard normal distribution by its mean and standard deviation, which will be denoted by $ \mu $ and $ \sigma $, respectively.

Moreover, since the maximum and minimum values of both $(\eD_{h}^{\text{ref}}, \sD_{h}^{\text{ref}})$ are known \textit{a priori} from the manufactured solution, the random noise generated for these data fields undergoes a min-max scaling process to ensure that the noise range aligns with that of the manufactured solution. Mathematically, the noise-added data fields can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\mathfrak{g}_{\text{noise}} &= \left[\frac{\mathfrak{g}_{\text{gn}} - \min(\mathfrak{g}_{\text{gn}})}{\max(\mathfrak{g}_{\text{gn}}) - \min(\mathfrak{g}_{\text{gn}})} \right] \left[\max(\mathfrak{g}) - \min(\mathfrak{g})\right] + \min(\mathfrak{g}), \\
		\mathfrak{s}_{\text{noise}} &= \left[\frac{\mathfrak{s}_{\text{gn}} - \min(\mathfrak{s}_{\text{gn}})}{\max(\mathfrak{s}_{\text{gn}}) - \min(\mathfrak{s}_{\text{gn}})} \right] \left[\max(\mathfrak{s}) - \min(\mathfrak{s})\right] + \min(\mathfrak{s}),
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $ \mathfrak{g}_{\text{gn}} $ and $ \mathfrak{s}_{\text{gn}} $ are vectors with components $ g_{i} $ and $ s_{i} $ that are independently drawn from a univariate normal distribution, i.e., $ g_{i}, s_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma) \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, d\} $. Finally, to control the magnitude of the introduced noise, we introduce a scalar parameter $ \alpha \in \mathbb{R} $, which scales $ \mathfrak{g}_{\text{noise}} $ and $ \mathfrak{s}_{\text{noise}} $. This parameter, $ \alpha $, will be referred to as the magnitude factor.

To carry out this study, we selected the DNLSP1-A formulation, also referred to as the natural one, and assumed a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and unit standard deviation. The number of sampled points was set as follows: \( N_{g} = 15 \) with three distinct values for \( N_{\theta} = \{5, 10, 20\} \). Additionally, four values for the magnitude factor were considered: \( \alpha = \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4\} \). This range begins with a very low level of noise and progressively increases to a higher level, where certain points may be regarded as outliers in the context of an experimental test. Finally, the finite element mesh was maintained as one hundred elements per coordinate direction, i.e. $ (N_{x}\times N_{y} = 100 \times 100) $.

Figure \ref{fig:compound_noise_u} presents the data-driven solutions obtained for the scalar pressure field. As shown, the data-driven solution closely approximates the expected analytical solution, even when subjected to a high-intensity noise dataset. However, it is noteworthy that for \( N_{\theta} = 5 \), there is a noticeable deviation between the data-driven pressure profiles and the exact solution for all values of \( \alpha \). This deviation can be attributed to the limited number of sampled points, as discussed in previous sections.

Figure \ref{fig:compound_noise_s}, on the other hand, displays the data-driven solutions for the flux vector field. At first glance, Figure \ref{fig:compound_noise_s}(a) might suggest that the Gaussian noise significantly impacts the accuracy of the data-driven solution for the flux vector field. However, a comparison with Figure \ref{fig:DNLSP1_A_plt_over_line_s}(e) reveals that the observed discrepancies between the data-driven solutions and the expected analytical solution are primarily due to the low number of sampled points. So, although the introduction of Gaussian noise seems to affect the flux vector field more than the scalar pressure field, the data-driven solutions still exhibit satisfactory accuracy, even when subjected to a high-intensity noise dataset.
