\section{Numerical experiments}

\subsection{Results for Darcy's problem}

In this section, we present the numerical results obtained using a data-driven approach to solve Darcy's fluid flow problem in porous media. To assess the accuracy of the proposed methodology, we employ a manufactured solution as a benchmark for comparison. So, we've select scalar potential field -- which in the Darcy's problem stands for the pressure, $ \uD $ -- consisting of: 

$$ 
\uD:\Omega \to \mathbb{R} \mid p(x,y) = \cos(\pi x)\cos(\pi y),
$$

where $ \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^{2} \mid \Omega = [0,1]\times[0,1] $. Thus, in light of equation \ref{eq:poisson_general} and the classical Darcy's model, quantities such as flux, $ \sD $, gradient, $ \eD $, and source term, $q$, can be easily derived by symbolic programming. By doing so and also assuming an isotropic medium with unitary constant porosity, $ \frac{k}{\mu} = 1 $, one can conclude that, for the selected pressure scalar function, the corresponding flux, pressure gradient and source term are given by:


\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		&\sD:\Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{2} &&\mid \sD = [-\pi\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y), -\pi \cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)]^{\top} \\
		&\eD:\Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{2} &&\mid \eD = [-\pi\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y), -\pi \cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)]^{\top} \\
		&q:\Omega \to \mathbb{R} &&\mid q = -2\pi^{2}\cos(\pi x) \cos(\pi y),
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

respectively. To obtain the discrete data-driven solutions, we set the total number of data samples as  $ N_{m} = N_{g} \times N_{\theta} = 2250 \times 2250 $, which theoretically represents the limit $ N_{m} \rightarrow \infty $. It should be noted that, since the relationship between  $ (\sD_h^{\text{ref}},\eD_h^{\text{ref}}) $  is known \textit{a priori} based on the manufactured solution, the discrete data relating flux and pressure gradient obtained from the uniaxial test, i.e $ \mathfrak{D}_m = \left\lbrace\left(\mathfrak{g}_n,\mathfrak{s}_n\right)\right\rbrace_{n \in \mathcal{N}_m} $, should ensure that the minimum and maximum values of both fields are encompassed. Regarding the finite element mesh, we used an intermediate fine mesh consisting of $ 200 \times 200 $ elements, i.e $ 200 $ elements per coordinate direction.    



Figures (\ref{fig:DNSLP1_sol_plt_over_line_u}a) to (\ref{fig:DNSLP1_sol_plt_over_line_u}e) illustrate the numerical solutions for the pressure field, $ \uD_h $, according to all the proposed data-driven formulations and the expected analytical solution is shown in figure (\ref{fig:DNSLP1_sol_plt_over_line_u}f). Based on the presented results, we observe that in the limit of $ N_{m} \rightarrow \infty $, all the data-driven formulations naturally recover the continuous or exact solution. This fact can be justified observing that the projection of the reference functions  $ \sD_h^{\text{ref}} $ and  $ \eD_h^{\text{ref}} $ onto  $ \text{DG}_0 $  minimizes the projection error in the sense of least squares, i.e:

$$
\| \sD_h^{\text{ref}} - \hat{\sD}_h \|_{L^2(\Omega)} \text{ and } \| \eD_h^{\text{ref}} - \hat{\eD}_h \|_{L^2(\Omega)}
$$

are minimized. Thus,  $ \hat{\sD}_h $  and  $ \hat{\eD}_h $  are the closest elementwise constant functions in  $ \text{DG}_0 $ to  $\sD_h^{\text{ref}} $  and  $ \eD_h^{\text{ref}} $ , respectively. Addionally, as  $ N_{m} \to \infty  $, the data  $ \mathfrak{D}_m $ becomes dense in the solution space, which means that the discrete data points   $ \left\lbrace\left(\mathfrak{g}_n,\mathfrak{s}_n\right)\right\rbrace_{n \in \mathcal{N}_m} $ will densely sample the range of  $ (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) $ . Consequently, the optimization problem ensures that:

$$
\| (\tilde{\sD}_h|_{K_n}, \tilde{\eD}_h|_{K_n}) - (\hat{\sD}_h|_{K_n}, \hat{\eD}_h|_{K_n}) \| \to 0 \text{ as } N_{m} \to \infty
$$

So,

\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		\| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \| &= \| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) + (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \|    \\
		&\leq \| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) \| + \| (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \|
	\end{aligned}.
\end{equation*}

Then, as $ N_{m} \to \infty $, $ \| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) \| \to 0  $ and considering that  $ \| (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \|  $ is already minimized, we conclude that the total error  $ \| (\tilde{\sD}_h, \tilde{\eD}_h) - (\sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}}) \|  $ also approaches zero.

\subsubsection{Effect of noise on the input data}

So far, we have studied convergence aspects regarding the data-driven formulation without introducing noise into the given data field $(\tilde{\eD}, \tilde{\sD})$. In the present section, in light of the proposed manufactured solution to Darcy's problem, we introduce a noise distribution in the original data field; in particular, a simple normal (or Gaussian) distribution was selected to introduce the random noise in to the data. The characteristics of the standard normal distribution will be defined by its mean and standard deviation, which will be denoted by $\mu_{\text{gn}} $ and $\sigma_{\text{gn}} $, respectively\footnote{The subscript $gn$ stands for Gaussian noise and is justified to avoid confusion with both the parameters $\mu$ - used to denote the viscosity of the fluid - and $\mD$ - used to denote the dual field of the  compatibility equation.}. 

As the maximum and minimum values of both $(\tilde{\eD}, \tilde{\sD})$ are completely known \textit{a priori}, the normal random noised generated for both data field was subjected to an min-max scaling process in order to adjust the noise range of data noise to be compatible with that found in the manufactured solution. Mathematical, we can express the noise data fields as:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\tilde{\eD}|_{\text{noise}} &=  \mathbf{g}_{\text{scaling}}  (\max(\tilde{\eD}) - \min(\tilde{\eD}) ) + \min(\tilde{\eD}), \ \mathbf{g}_{\text{scaling}} = \dfrac{\mathbf{g}_{\text{gn}} - \min(\textbf{g}_{gn})}{\max(\textbf{g}_{gn}) - \min(\textbf{g}_{gn})} \\
		\tilde{\sD}|_{\text{noise}} &=  \mathbf{s}_{\text{scaling}}  (\max(\tilde{\sD}) - \min(\tilde{\sD}) ) + \min(\tilde{\sD}), \quad \mathbf{s}_{\text{scaling}} = \dfrac{\mathbf{s}_{\text{gn}} - \min(\textbf{s}_{gn})}{\max(\textbf{s}_{gn}) - \min(\textbf{s}_{gn})} \\
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where, $ \mathbf{g}_{\text{gn}} $ and $ \mathbf{s}_{\text{gn}} $ are vectors whose components $ g_{i} $ and $ s_{i} $ respectively, are independently drawn from a univariate normal distribution, i.e. $ g_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{gn}},\sigma_{\text{gn}})$ and $ s_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{gn}},\sigma_{\text{gn}}), \forall i \in \{1,\dots,d\}$. Finally, in order to control the magnitude of generated noisy data, we introduce a scalar parameter, $ \alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, which multiplies $ \tilde{\eD}|_{\text{noise }} $ and $ \tilde{\sD}|_{\text{noise}} $, so from now on we'll denote $ \alpha $ as a noise control factor. 


In the first experiment with noisy data fields, three different values of the pair $ (N_{g},N_{\theta}) $ were considered, and the following parameters $ \mu_{\text{gn}} = 0.0 $, $ \sigma_{\text{gn}} = 1.0 $ and $ \alpha = 2.5 \times 10^{-2} $ were adopted to introduce the random normal noise data. Figure \ref{fig:orig_flux_grad_samples} illustrates the relationship between $ \|\tilde{\sD} \|_{2} $ and $ \|\tilde{\eD}\|_{2} $ for the three selected values of $ (N_{g},N_{\theta}) $. The choice for those values intends to characterize distinct data scenarios: the first one (figure \ref{fig:orig_flux_grad_samples_NbcNth_4}) should represent absence (or lack) of data; the second one (figure \ref{fig:orig_flux_grad_samples_NbcNth_8}) should represent an intermediate case, where an reasonable number os points is provided, and the last one (figure \ref{fig:orig_flux_grad_samples_NbcNth_16}) should depict an ideal case, where a large amount of data is given.
