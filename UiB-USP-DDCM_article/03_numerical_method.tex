\section{Numerical method}

Two different problems are used to illustrate the behavior
of the data-driven formulation introduced in the previous section,
namely, the prototypical elliptic second order Poisson's problem, that
models flow in porous media or heat conduction, which is an example of 
a problem with scalar unknown and on the other hand, the classical
elasticity problem which is an example of an elliptic problem with
vector unknown. We present the particular form
of the NLSP1 formulation presented above for these
two problems and propose some discrete versions of it
obtained by making different choices of spaces associated
to finite element partitions of the computational domain.

\subsection{Darcy's problem}

With reference to the general formulation we have presented in the
previous section, let us rewrite Darcy's problem with a more usual
notation specific of flow in porous media by redefining the primal
unknowns, namely the flux, the potential and the gradient
as follows
\begin{itemize}
 \item Flux unknown: $s \leftarrow \sD$
 \item Scalar unknown: $u \leftarrow \uD$
 \item Gradient unknown: $e \leftarrow \eD$
\end{itemize}
whereas for the dual fields we adopt
\begin{itemize}
 \item Mass balance constraint: $\lambda \leftarrow \lD$
 \item Compatibility constraint: $\mu \leftarrow \mD$
\end{itemize}

Let us first recall the standard or reference formulation for this
problem in its mixed form with pure Dirichlet
boundary conditions: Find pressure $\uD$ and flux $\sD$ such that
$$
\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{rcll}
\nabla \cdot \sD &=& q & \mbox{in}~\Omega,\\
\\
\eD &=& \nabla{p} & \mbox{in}~\Omega \\
\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\sD,\eD) &=& 0 & \mbox{in}~\Omega \\
\\
\uD &=& p_0(\mathbf{x}) & \mbox{on}~\partial\Omega
\end{array}
\right.
$$
in which $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}$ stands for the flux law.
In the simplest case, we have the classical Darcy's model
$$
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\sD,\eD) = \sD  + \dfrac{\mathbf{K}}{\mu} \eD = 0\\
$$
being $\mathbf{K}$ the permeability tensor and $\mu$ the viscosity of the fluid.
We choose to write the formulation in this way to be able
to handle more complicated non-linear cases in which the flux
can not be obtained explicitly in terms of the pressure gradient,
e.g., in the Forcheimer's model
$$
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\sD,\eD) = \eD + \mu\,\mathbf{K}^{-1}\,\sD +
\beta \,\|\sD \|\, \sD = 0
$$
where $\| \sD \| = (\sD \cdot \sD)^{1/2}$. 
Other non-linear models can also be considered. 
The standard problem will be solved for comparison purposes,
to produce reference solutions
and also to obtain plausible synthetic data to be used in the
data-driven case. This problem will be solved by a suitable
finite element formulation when needed throughout the article. 

\subsection{Discrete data-driven formulations for Darcy's problem}

Let us first recall the base formulation from which several discrete versions 
will be derived. We take as starting point the formulation NLSP1 specialized for 
Darcy's problem: Given the data fields $(\tilde{\eD}, \tilde{\sD})$ we seek to
find primal fields
$\uD \in H_0^1(\Omega)$, $\sD \in L^2(\Omega)$, $\eD \in L^2(\Omega)$
and dual fields $\lD \in H_0^1(\Omega)$, $\mD \in L^2(\Omega)$
that minimize
\begin{multline}
\Phi_{\lD \mD}((\lD, \mD), (\uD, \eD, \sD); q, \Omega) +
\frac{1}{2}\|\eD-\tilde{\eD}\|^2_{L^2}+
\frac{1}{2}\|\sD-\tilde{\sD}\|^{2}_{L^{2}} =  \\
- \int_{\Omega} \sD \cdot \nabla \lD \, d\mathbf{x} - \int_{\Omega} \lD \, q \, d\mathbf{x} + \int_{\Omega} \mD \cdot (\eD -  \nabla \uD)\,d\mathbf{x}
+ \\
+ \frac{1}{2} \int_{\Omega} \left[ (\sD - \tilde{\sD}) \cdot (\sD - \tilde{\sD}) + (\eD - \tilde{\eD}) \cdot (\eD - \tilde{\eD}) \right] \, d\mathbf{x},
\end{multline}
The concrete problem to be solved in this case is obtained
by taking variations of this functional with respect to all fields
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \sD \cdot (\sD - \tilde{\sD}) \, d\mathbf{x} - \int_{\Omega}(\delta \sD \cdot \nabla \lD ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\quad \forall \delta \sD \in  L^2(\Omega) \\
                 - \int_{\Omega}( \mD \cdot  \nabla \delta \uD ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\quad \forall \delta \uD \in H_0^1(\Omega) \\
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \eD \cdot (\eD - \tilde{\eD}) \, d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} (\delta \eD \cdot \mD) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\quad \forall \delta \eD \in  L^2(\Omega) \\
                -\int_{\Omega} \big(\sD \cdot \nabla \delta \lD + q \, \delta \lambda \big) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\quad \forall \delta \lD \in H_0^1(\Omega) \\
                \int_{\Omega} \delta \mD \cdot (\eD -  \nabla \uD) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\quad \forall \delta \mD \in  L^2(\Omega)
        \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The discrete variational formulation follows by introducing a finite element partition of
the computational domain $\Omega$, the mesh, $\mathcal{T}_h = \{K_i\}_{i=1}^N$
such that $\overline{\Omega} \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} \overline{K}_i$ and taking
discrete spaces
$V_{\uD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \in H_0^1(\Omega)$, $V_{\sD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \in L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\eD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \in L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\lD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \in H_0^1(\Omega)$
and $V_{\mD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \in L^2(\Omega)$.
Let us introduce the following finite element spaces for further use:
\begin{eqnarray}
DG_k(\mathcal{T}_h) & = &  \left \{ v \in L^2(\Omega),~ v|_K \in P_k(K)~\forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h \right \}\\
CG_k(\mathcal{T}_h) & = &  \left \{ v \in H^1(\Omega),~ v|_K \in P_k(K)~\forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h,~v = v_D~\mbox{em}~\partial{\Omega} \right \}\\
RT_k(\mathcal{T}_h) & = & \\
BDM_k(\mathcal{T}_h) &= &
\end{eqnarray}
The corresponding space for $d$-vector valued functions will be
$[DG_k(\mathcal{T}_h)]^d$ and $[CG_k(\mathcal{T}_h)]^d$.
For simplicity of notation,
in the forthcoming parts we omit the dependence of these
spaces with respect to the mesh $\mathcal{T}_h$.

\subsubsection{Distribution of data over $\Omega$}

Prior to presenting some discrete versions of the previous
formulation, a discussion on the distribution of data across the domain is in order.
This step is crucial as it significantly influences the data-driven solution. In our ultimate goal of solving the global optimization problem, determining the appropriate data distribution, specifically the domain decomposition, remains an unknown and significant challenge. This issue will be addressed in future works.
In this article, we establish a fixed distribution of the available data over the domain $\Omega$. To illustrate our approach, we will consider Darcy's problem as an example and follow these steps:

\begin{itemize}
 \item[1.] Compute a reference solution
 $(\uD_h^{\text{ref}}, \sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}})$
 by using a standard finite element discretization of the governing
 equations. For this step, assume a known constitutive law. 
 In practice, the law may be a known analytical function that fits, i.e.,
 a reconstructed constitutive manifold from the available data.
 Depending on the discretization adopted, $\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ 
 and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}}$ may be obtained by post-processing
 of $\uD_h^{\text{ref}}$.;
 
 \item[2.] Compute elementwise constant functions 
 $\hat{\sD}_h$ and $\hat{\eD}_h$ 
 by projecting $\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}}$
 onto $\DG_0$, i.e., find $\hat{\sD}_h$ such that
 $$
\int_{\Omega} \hat{{\sD}}_h \cdot {\mathbf{v}} \,{\dx} =
\int_{\Omega} {{\sD}}_h^{\text{ref}} \cdot {\mathbf{v}} \,{\dx} ~~\forall {\mathbf{v}} \in DG_0
 $$
 and similarly for $\hat{\eD}_h$. Note that depending on the choice
 of finite element spaces to obtain the reference solution
 (e.g. $\uD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \CG_1,~\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \DG_0$), 
 the projection above may be trivially obtained.

 \item[3.] Compute functions $\tilde{\sD}_h$ and $\tilde{\eD}_h$ in $\DG_0$
 by minimizing the Euclidean distance in $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$ from the available data
 $\mathfrak{D}_m =\left\lbrace\left(\mathfrak{g}_n,\mathfrak{s}_n\right)\right\rbrace_{n \in \mathcal{N}_m}$
 to the reference elementwise constant solution $(\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h)$, i.e., for $K_n \in \mathcal{T}_h$
 %if we define $\hat{\mathbf{D}} = \left \{ (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) \right \}_{n=1}^{n_\text{el}}$ $\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{el}} \times 2d}$ to be the matrix whose line $n$ is formed by the numerical values of  $(\left. \hat{\sD}_h\right |_{K_n}, \left. \hat{\eD}_h\right |_{K_n}),$ then, for $K_n \in \mathcal{T}_h$
 \begin{equation*}
 (\left. \tilde{\sD}_h\right |_{K_n}, \left. \tilde{\eD}_h\right |_{K_n}) =
 \argminA_{\left(\mathfrak{g}_k,\mathfrak{s}_k\right) \in \mathfrak{D}_m} \, \text{dist} \left ( (\left. \hat{\sD}_h\right |_{K_n}, \left. \hat{\eD}_h\right |_{K_n}) \, , \, \left(\mathfrak{g}_k,\mathfrak{s}_k\right) \right )
\end{equation*}
Note that with these choices, the skeleton of the associated 
domain decomposition conforms to the element edges/faces of the mesh.
\end{itemize}
Let us illustrate with an example the procedure described above.
Suppose we have the discrete data relating flux and pressure gradient
obtained from a typical uniaxial test shown in Figure \ref{fig:datadistproc}.
As illustrated in the figure, the data exhibits some noise around the mean curve, which is depicted in red. Based on prior knowledge of the problem, Forchheimer's model, introduced earlier, is expected to be applicable. Therefore, we can perform a linear regression on the experimental data. For this 1D setting, we adjust the coefficients 
$\mu/k$ and $\beta$ in
$$
g + \dfrac{\mu}{k}\,s + \beta \,s^2 = 0
$$
where $g = \|\eD\|$ and $s = \|\sD\|$ are the magnitude of the
pressure gradient and flux, respectively. 
However, if such prior informations are not available, a suitable model
must be proposed. With this reconstructed manifold we may solve Darcy's problem. 
To that end we also make the assumption that the medium is isotropic in this example,
so the permeability tensor can be written as $\mathbf{K} = k \, \mathbf{I}_{d\times d}$ 
and consider the following finite element formulation to solve the reference
(non linear) problem: Find $\uD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \CG_{k}$ and
$\sD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \RT_{k+1}^{\text{disc}}$ such that
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
\int_{\Omega} { \left [ \eD_h^{\text{ref}} + \frac{\mu}{k}\,\sD_h^{\text{ref}} +
\beta \,r(\sD_h^{\text{ref}} )\, \sD_h^{\text{ref}} \right ]} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}_h \, d\mathbf{x} & = & 0 &~~ \forall \boldsymbol{\tau}_h \in \RT_{k+1} \\
\int_{\Omega} {\sD^{\text{ref}} \cdot \nabla{v}_h} \, d\mathbf{x} & = & 0 &~~ \forall v_h \in \CG^{0}_k
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where
$$
r( \sD ) = \eta \left [  \left (1 + \dfrac{\sD \cdot \sD}{\eta^2}\right )^{\frac12} - 1 \right ]
$$
is a regularization of $\| \sD \|$ introduced to avoid a singular Jacobian
during the Newton-Raphson iterations used for solving the nonlinearites. 
This regularization $r(\|\sD \|) \rightarrow \|\sD \|$ as $\eta \rightarrow 0$. 
In practice, we take $\eta = 10^{-12}$. The reference solution is shown 
in figure \ref{fig:refsol}. 

The procedure illustrated above is similar for the elasticity problem, 
except that the functions belong to higher dimensional spaces.

\subsubsection{A first discrete data-driven formulation}

The first discrete formulation results from a natural choice
of finite element spaces for the different fields, namely,
$\CG_1 \subset H^1(\Omega)$ for the scalar fields $\uD$ and $\lD$
and $[\DG_0]^d \subset L^2(\Omega)$ for the vector-valued
fields $\eD$, $\sD$ and $\mD$. Once the data have been
distributed over the domain according to the procedure
explained above so as to produce fields $\tilde{\sD}_h$
and $\tilde{\eD}_h$, the discrete formulation can be stated
as: Find primal fields
$(\uD_h,\eD_h, \sD_h, \lD_h, \mD_h ) \in \CG_1\times [\DG]^d \times [\DG]^d
\times \CG_1 \times [\DG]^d$ such that
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \sD_h \cdot (\sD_h - \tilde{\sD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x} -
                \int_{\Omega}(\delta \sD_h \cdot \nabla \lD_h ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                 - \int_{\Omega}( \mD_h \cdot  \nabla \delta \uD_h ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \eD_h \cdot (\eD_h - \tilde{\eD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} (\delta \eD_h \cdot \mD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
                -\int_{\Omega} \big(\sD_h \cdot \nabla \delta \lD_h + q \, \delta \lambda_h \big) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                \int_{\Omega} \delta \mD_h \cdot (\eD_h -  \nabla \uD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &
        \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$\forall (\delta\uD_h,\delta\eD_h, \delta\sD_h, \delta\lD_h, \delta\mD_h ) \in \CG_1\times [\DG]^d \times [\DG]^d
\times \CG_1 \times [\DG]^d$.
This formulation is easily solved in the open source finite element platform
Firedrake \cite{firedrake} we have chosen for implementation.
