\section{Numerical method and Implementation}

Two different problems are used to illustrate the behavior of the data-driven 
formulation introduced in the previous section. The first one is the 
prototypical elliptic second-order Poisson problem, which models Darcy's flow 
in porous media or heat conduction and serves as an example of a problem with 
scalar unknown. The second one is the classical elasticity problem, 
as an example of an elliptic problem with vector unknown. We present the 
specific form of the NLSP1 formulation for these two problems and propose some 
discrete versions of it, obtained by making different choices of spaces associated 
with finite element partitions of the computational domain.

\subsection{Darcy's problem}

With reference to the general formulation we have presented in the
previous section, let us rewrite Darcy's problem with a more usual
notation specific of flow in porous media by redefining the primal
unknowns, namely the flux, the potential and the gradient
as $(u, s, e) \leftarrow (\uD, \sD, \eD)$, whereas for the dual fields,
the mass balance and compatibility constraint $(\lambda, \mu) \leftarrow (\lD, \mD)$.
Let us first recall the standard or reference formulation for this
problem in its mixed form with pure Dirichlet
boundary conditions: Find pressure $\uD$ and flux $\sD$ such that
$$
\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{rcll}
\nabla \cdot \sD &=& q & \mbox{in}~\Omega,\\
\\
\eD &=& \nabla{p} & \mbox{in}~\Omega \\
\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\sD,\eD) &=& 0 & \mbox{in}~\Omega \\
\\
\uD &=& \uD_0(\mathbf{x}) & \mbox{on}~\partial\Omega
\end{array}
\right.
$$
in which $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}$ stands for the flux law.
In the simplest case, we have the classical Darcy's model
$$
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\sD,\eD) = \sD  + \dfrac{\mathbf{K}}{\mu} \eD = 0\\
$$
being $\mathbf{K}$ the permeability tensor and $\mu$ the viscosity of the fluid.
We choose to write the formulation in this way to be able
to handle more complicated non-linear cases in which the flux
can not be obtained explicitly in terms of the pressure gradient,
e.g., in the Forcheimer's model
$$
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\sD,\eD) = \eD + \mu\,\mathbf{K}^{-1}\,\sD +
\beta \,\|\sD \|\, \sD = 0
$$
where $\| \sD \| = (\sD \cdot \sD)^{1/2}$. 
Other non-linear models can also be considered. 
The standard problem will be solved for comparison purposes,
to produce reference solutions
and also to obtain plausible synthetic data to be used in the
data-driven case. This problem will be solved by a suitable
finite element formulation when needed throughout the article. 

\subsubsection{Data-driven formulations for Darcy's problem}

Let us first recall the base formulation from which several discrete versions 
will be derived. We take as starting point formulation NLSP1 specialized for 
Darcy's problem. For the proximity measures we take as a particular
case the $L^2(\Omega)$-norm, i.e.,
$$
\varphi_{\eD}(\eD - \tilde{\eD}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\eD-\tilde{\eD}\|^2_{L^2},~~~
\varphi_{\sD}(\sD - \tilde{\sD}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\sD-\tilde{\sD}\|^2_{L^2}
$$
For simplicity we present the case with pure Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Given the data fields $(\tilde{\eD}, \tilde{\sD})$ we seek to
find primal fields
$\uD \in H^1(\Omega)$, $\sD \in L^2(\Omega)$, $\eD \in [L^2(\Omega)]^d$
and dual fields $\lD \in H^1(\Omega)$, $\mD \in [L^2(\Omega)]^2$,
$\uD = \uD_D(\mathbf{x})$ and $\lD = 0$ for $\mathbf{x} \in \partial{\Omega}$,
that minimize
\begin{multline}
\mathcal{L} = \Phi_{\lD \mD}((\lD, \mD), (\uD, \eD, \sD); q, \Omega) +
\varphi_{\eD}(\eD - \tilde{\eD}) + \varphi_{\sD}(\sD - \tilde{\sD}) =  \\
- \int_{\Omega} \sD \cdot \nabla \lD \, d\mathbf{x} - \int_{\Omega} \lD \, q \, d\mathbf{x} + \int_{\Omega} \mD \cdot (\eD -  \nabla \uD)\,d\mathbf{x}
+ \\
+ \frac{1}{2} \int_{\Omega} \left[ (\sD - \tilde{\sD}) \cdot (\sD - \tilde{\sD}) + (\eD - \tilde{\eD}) \cdot (\eD - \tilde{\eD}) \right] \, d\mathbf{x},
\end{multline}
The concrete problem to be solved in this case is obtained
by taking variations of this functional with respect to all fields
and writing the optimality conditions as we have done in Section \ref{sec:solvability}
\begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
               \text{d}_{\sD}\mathcal{L}  & = \underbrace{\int_{\Omega}  \delta \sD \cdot (\sD - \tilde{\sD}) \, d\mathbf{x}}_{\text{d}\varphi_{\sD}(\sD,\tilde{\sD}; \delta{\sD})} - \int_{\Omega}(\delta \sD \cdot \nabla \lD ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = & ~0 &\quad \forall \delta \sD \in  L^2(\Omega), \\
                 \text{d}_{\eD}\mathcal{L} & = - \int_{\Omega}( \mD \cdot  \nabla \delta \uD ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = & ~0 &\quad \forall \delta \uD \in H_0^1(\Omega), \\
                \text{d}_{\eD}\mathcal{L} & = \underbrace{\int_{\Omega}  \delta \eD \cdot (\eD - \tilde{\eD}) \, d\mathbf{x}}_{\text{d}\varphi_{\eD}(\eD,\tilde{\eD}; \delta{\eD})}  + \int_{\Omega} (\delta \eD \cdot \mD) \, d\mathbf{x} & = & ~0 &\quad \forall \delta \eD \in  L^2(\Omega), \\
                \text{d}_{\lD}\mathcal{L} & = -\int_{\Omega} \big(\sD \cdot \nabla \delta \lD + q \, \delta \lambda \big) \, d\mathbf{x} & = & ~0 &\quad \forall \delta \lD \in H_0^1(\Omega), \\
                \text{d}_{\mD}\mathcal{L} & = \int_{\Omega} \delta \mD \cdot (\eD -  \nabla \uD) \, d\mathbf{x} & = & ~0 &\quad \forall \delta \mD \in  L^2(\Omega).
        \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
\begin{remark}
In this part of the article, we have adopted a less abstract notation to better establish 
the connection with the implementation in the open-source finite element platform
Firedrake \cite{firedrake}. The implementation details are provided later on. 
A notable feature of the Firedrake platform is its simplicity, which allows
for straightforward testing of different formulations and finite element spaces.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Discrete formulation for Darcy's problem}

The discrete variational formulation follows by introducing a finite element partition of
the computational domain $\Omega$, the mesh, $\mathcal{T}_h = \{K_i\}_{i=1}^N$
such that $\overline{\Omega} \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} \overline{K}_i$ and taking
discrete spaces
$V_{\uD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset H_0^1(\Omega)$, $V_{\sD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\eD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\lD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset H_0^1(\Omega)$
and $V_{\mD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$.
Let us introduce the following finite element spaces for further use:
\begin{eqnarray}
\DG_k(\mathcal{T}_h) & = &  \left \{ v \in L^2(\Omega),~ v|_K \in P_k(K)~\forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h \right \}\\
\CG_k^D(\mathcal{T}_h) & = &  \left \{ v \in H^1(\Omega),~ v|_K \in P_k(K)~\forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h,~v = u_D~\mbox{on}~\partial{\Omega} \right \}~~~~\\
\RT_k(\mathcal{T}_h) & = & \left \{ \mathbf{v} \in \Hdiv(\text{div};\Omega) : \mathbf{v} |_{E} \in RT_k (K)~\forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h \right \},\\
\BDM_k(\mathcal{T}_h) &= & \left \{ \mathbf{v} \in \Hdiv(\text{div};\Omega) : \mathbf{v} |_{E} \in BDM_k (K)~\forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h \right \}
\end{eqnarray}
where $RT_k(K)$ is the local Raviart-Thomas space and $BDM_k (K)$ 
is the local Brezzi-Douglas-Marini space of order $k$ (see \cite{RT,BDM}).
The corresponding space for $d$-vector valued functions will be
denoted by $[DG_k(\mathcal{T}_h)]^d$ and $[CG_k(\mathcal{T}_h)]^d$
and the space with homogeneous Dirichlet data by
$\CG_k^0(\mathcal{T}_h)$. For simplicity of notation,
in the forthcoming parts we omit the dependence of these
spaces with respect to the mesh $\mathcal{T}_h$.
Prior to presenting some discrete versions of the NLSP1
formulation, a discussion on the distribution of data across
the domain is in order.

\subsubsection{Distribution of data over $\Omega$} \label{sec:datadist}

This step is crucial as it significantly influences the data-driven solution. 
In our ultimate goal of solving the global optimization problem, determining
the appropriate data distribution, specifically the domain decomposition, 
remains an unknown and significant challenge. This issue will be addressed in future works.
In this article, we establish a fixed distribution of the available data over the domain $\Omega$. 
To illustrate our approach, we will consider Darcy's problem as an example and follow these steps:

\begin{itemize}
 \item[1.] Compute a reference solution
 $(\uD_h^{\text{ref}}, \sD_h^{\text{ref}}, \eD_h^{\text{ref}})$
 by using a standard finite element discretization of the governing
 equations. For this step, assume a known constitutive law. 
 In practice, the law may be a known analytical function that fits the data, i.e.,
 a reconstructed constitutive manifold from the available data.
 Depending on the discretization adopted, $\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ 
 and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}}$ may be obtained by post-processing
 of $\uD_h^{\text{ref}}$.;
 
 \item[2.] Compute elementwise constant functions 
 $\hat{\sD}_h$ and $\hat{\eD}_h$ 
 by projecting $\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}}$
 onto $\DG_0$, i.e., find $\hat{\sD}_h$ such that
 $$
\int_{\Omega} \hat{{\sD}}_h \cdot {\mathbf{v}} \,{\dx} =
\int_{\Omega} {{\sD}}_h^{\text{ref}} \cdot {\mathbf{v}} \,{\dx} ~~\forall {\mathbf{v}} \in \DG_0
 $$
 and similarly for $\hat{\eD}_h$. Note that depending on the choice
 of finite element spaces to obtain the reference solution
 (e.g. $\uD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \CG_1,~\sD_h^{\text{ref}}$ and $\eD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \DG_0$), 
 the projection above may be trivially obtained.

 \item[3.] Compute functions $\tilde{\sD}_h$ and $\tilde{\eD}_h$ in $\DG_0$
 by minimizing the distance (e.g., the Euclidean distance)
 in $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$ from the available data
 $\mathfrak{D}_m =\left\lbrace\left(\mathfrak{g}_n,\mathfrak{s}_n\right)\right\rbrace_{n \in \mathcal{N}_m}$
 to the reference elementwise constant solution $(\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h)$, i.e., for $K_n \in \mathcal{T}_h$
 %if we define $\hat{\mathbf{D}} = \left \{ (\hat{\sD}_h, \hat{\eD}_h) \right \}_{n=1}^{n_\text{el}}$ $\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{el}} \times 2d}$ to be the matrix whose line $n$ is formed by the numerical values of  $(\left. \hat{\sD}_h\right |_{K_n}, \left. \hat{\eD}_h\right |_{K_n}),$ then, for $K_n \in \mathcal{T}_h$
 \begin{equation*}
 (\left. \tilde{\sD}_h\right |_{K_n}, \left. \tilde{\eD}_h\right |_{K_n}) =
 \argminA_{\left(\mathfrak{g}_k,\mathfrak{s}_k\right) \in \mathfrak{D}_m} \, \text{dist} \left ( (\left. \hat{\sD}_h\right |_{K_n}, \left. \hat{\eD}_h\right |_{K_n}) \, , \, \left(\mathfrak{g}_k,\mathfrak{s}_k\right) \right )
\end{equation*}
Note that with these choices, the skeleton of the associated 
domain decomposition conforms to the element edges/faces of the mesh.
\end{itemize}
\begin{remark}
The previous procedure can be applied similarly to the elasticity problem, 
except that the functions belong to higher dimensional spaces. 
In particular $u \leftarrow \uE$ is a $d$-vector valued function and
$e \leftarrow \eE$ and $s \leftarrow \sE$ are symmetric
$d\times d$-tensor valued functions. 
Therefore, we minimize the distance in $\mathbb{R}^{n},~n = d\,(d+1)$.
\end{remark}
Let us illustrate with an example the procedure described above for Darcy's problem.
Suppose we have the discrete data relating flux and pressure gradient
obtained from a typical uniaxial test shown in Figure \ref{fig:datadistproc}.
As illustrated in the figure, the data exhibits some noise around the mean curve, which is depicted in red. Based on prior knowledge of the problem, Forchheimer's model, introduced earlier, is expected to be applicable. Therefore, we can perform a linear regression on the experimental data. For this 1D setting, we adjust the coefficients 
$\mu/k$ and $\beta$ in
$$
g + \dfrac{\mu}{k}\,s + \beta \,s^2 = 0
$$
where $\eD = g \, \check{\mathbf{e}_y}$ and $\sD = s \, \check{\mathbf{e}_y}$,
with $g$ and $s$ being the magnitudes of the pressure gradient and flux, respectively. 
However, if such prior informations are not available, a suitable model
must be proposed. With this reconstructed manifold we may solve Darcy's problem. 
To that end we also make the assumption that the medium is isotropic in this example,
so the permeability tensor can be written as $\mathbf{K} = k \, \mathbf{I}_{d\times d}$ 
and consider the following finite element formulation to solve the reference
(non linear) problem: Find $\uD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \CG^D_{k}$ and
$\sD_h^{\text{ref}} \in \RT_{k+1}^{\text{disc}}$ such that
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
\int_{\Omega} { \left [ \eD_h^{\text{ref}} + \frac{\mu}{k}\,\sD_h^{\text{ref}} +
\beta \,r(\sD_h^{\text{ref}} )\, \sD_h^{\text{ref}} \right ]} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}_h \, d\mathbf{x} & = & 0 &~~ \forall \boldsymbol{\tau}_h \in \RT_{k+1}^{\text{disc}} \\
\int_{\Omega} {\sD^{\text{ref}} \cdot \nabla{v}_h} \, d\mathbf{x} & = & 0 &~~ \forall v_h \in \CG^{0}_k
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where
$$
r( \sD ) = \eta \left [  \left (1 + \dfrac{\sD \cdot \sD}{\eta^2}\right )^{\frac12} - 1 \right ]
$$
is a regularization of $\| \sD \|$, such that 
$r(\|\sD \|) \rightarrow \|\sD \|$ as $\eta \rightarrow 0$. The regularization is
introduced to ensure convergence of the Newton-Raphson iterations 
used for solving the nonlinearites. In practice, we take $\eta = 10^{-12}$. 
The reference solution, namely, the pressure, gradient and
flux fields are shown in figure \ref{fig:refsol}. Figure \ref{fig:domains} shows the associated 
domain decomposition resulting from the data distribution procedure
described in \ref{sec:datadist}. Each color corresponds to a specific point in the data
set $\mathfrak{D}_m$. This simply means that in all elements $K \in \mathcal{T}_h$ marked with
the same color, the corresponding point in the data set will be used to compute
the data driven solution.
Finally, figure \ref{fig:soldd} shows the corresponding 
data-driven solution which was obtained with the discrete formulation 
to be presented in the next section. As shown in the figure the behavior
of the data-driven solution reflects the sparsity of the available data.
This changes significantly if we increase the number of points in the data set
as shown in figure \ref{fig:solddmorepoints}. 
This initial example already illustrates some of the key components 
involved in the data-driven computational paradigm. 
The procedure outlined above can serve as an effective method for initializing
the global optimization problem that we ultimately aim to solve. 
Now, we proceed to formulate discrete versions of NLSP1.

\subsection{Discrete data-driven formulations for Darcy's problem}

The first discrete formulation results from a natural choice
of finite element spaces for the different fields, namely,
$\CG_1 \subset H^1(\Omega)$ for the scalar fields $\uD$ and $\lD$
and $[\DG_0]^d \subset L^2(\Omega)$ for the vector-valued
fields $\eD$, $\sD$ and $\mD$. Once the data have been
distributed over the domain according to the procedure
explained above so as to produce fields $\tilde{\sD}_h$
and $\tilde{\eD}_h$, the discrete formulation can be stated
as: Find primal fields
$(\uD_h,\eD_h, \sD_h, \lD_h, \mD_h ) \in \CG^D_{k+1}\times [\DG]_k^d \times [\DG]_k^d
\times \CG^0_{k+1} \times [\DG]_k^d$, $k \ge 0$, such that
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \sD_h \cdot (\sD_h - \tilde{\sD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x} -
                \int_{\Omega}(\delta \sD_h \cdot \nabla \lD_h ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                 - \int_{\Omega}( \mD_h \cdot  \nabla \delta \uD_h ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \eD_h \cdot (\eD_h - \tilde{\eD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} (\delta \eD_h \cdot \mD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
                -\int_{\Omega} \big(\sD_h \cdot \nabla \delta \lD_h + q \, \delta \lambda_h \big) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                \int_{\Omega} \delta \mD_h \cdot (\eD_h -  \nabla \uD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &
        \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$\forall (\delta\uD_h,\delta\eD_h, \delta\sD_h, \delta\lD_h, \delta\mD_h ) \in \CG^0_{k+1}\times \VDG_{k} \times \VDG_{k}
\times \CG^0_k \times \VDG_{k}$.
This discrete formulation will be termed DNLSP1-A. This is 
the formulation we have used to solve the example above.

Another natural choice of spaces we can consider for this problem is to use 
the same interpolation for all fields, i.e., 
$(\uD_h,\eD_h, \sD_h, \lD_h, \mD_h ) \in \CG_{k+1 }\times \VCG_{k+1} \times \VCG_{k+1}
\times \CG_{k+1} \times \VCG_{k+1}$, resulting in an equal order formulation. 
We expect this formulation to fail due to the structure of the saddle point problems 
being solved. This equal order case will be named DNLSP1-Aeqo and will be assessed 
in the results section to compare with other variants.

\subsection{Some variants of the discrete data-driven formulation}

We can derive three other formulations by integrating by parts some 
of the terms in the base formulation NLSP1 and making suitable 
choices of discrete finite element spaces. 

\subsubsection{Formulation DNLSP1-B}

Let us begin with the functional $\Phi_{\lD \mD}$ and integrate
both terms in the balance and compatibility constraint, i.e.,
\begin{multline}
\Phi_{\lD \mD}((\lD, \mD), (\uD, \eD, \sD); q, \Omega) =  \\
 \int_{\Omega}  \lD \left ( \nabla \cdot \sD - q \right ) \, d\mathbf{x} + 
\int_{\Omega} \mD \cdot \eD\,d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} \uD \, \nabla \cdot \mD \,d\mathbf{x}
- \int_{\partial{\Omega}} {\uD_D (\mD \cdot \check{\mathbf{n}})} \, d\Gamma \label{eq:Phi5B}
\end{multline}
where $\uD_D$ is the Dirichlet data for $\uD$ and $\lD = 0$ on $\partial{\Omega}$.
Note that the latter conditions are imposed weakly in (\ref{eq:Phi5B}).
Natural choices for the spaces are now
$V_{\uD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\sD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset \Hdiv(\text{div};\Omega)$, $V_{\eD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset \Hdiv(\text{div};\Omega)$, $V_{\lD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$
and $V_{\mD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset \Hdiv(\text{div};\Omega)$.
Hence, the discrete variational problem we propose for this case reads:
Find primal fields
$(\uD_h,\eD_h, \sD_h, \lD_h, \mD_h ) \in \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1} \times \RT_{k+1}
\times \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1}$, $k \ge 0$, such that
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \sD_h \cdot (\sD_h - \tilde{\sD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x} +
                \int_{\Omega} {\lD_h \nabla \cdot \delta \sD_h } \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                 \int_{\Omega}(\delta \uD_h \nabla \cdot \mD_h  ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \eD_h \cdot (\eD_h - \tilde{\eD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} (\delta \eD_h \cdot \mD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
                \int_{\Omega} \delta \lD_h \left ( \nabla \cdot  \sD_h  -  q \right ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                \int_{\Omega}{ \delta \mD_h \cdot \eD_h}\, d\mathbf{x} +  \int_{\Omega}{\uD_h \nabla  \cdot \delta \mD_h}\, d\mathbf{x}
              - \int_{\partial{\Omega}}{ \uD_D (\delta{\mD}_h \cdot \check{\mathbf{n}})}\,d\Gamma & = 0, &
        \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$\forall (\delta\uD_h,\delta\eD_h, \delta\sD_h, \delta\lD_h, \delta\mD_h ) \in  \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1} \times \RT_{k+1}
\times \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1}$.
This discrete formulation will be termed DNLSP1-B. 

\subsubsection{Formulation DNLSP1-C}

The third option corresponds to integrate by parts only the compatibility term,
resulting the functional $\Phi_{\lD \mD}$
\begin{multline}
\Phi_{\lD \mD}((\lD, \mD), (\uD, \eD, \sD); q, \Omega) =  \\
- \int_{\Omega} \sD \cdot \nabla \lD \, d\mathbf{x} - \int_{\Omega} \lD \, q \, d\mathbf{x} + 
\int_{\Omega} \mD \cdot \eD\,d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} \uD \, \nabla \cdot \mD \,d\mathbf{x}
- \int_{\partial{\Omega}} {\uD_D (\mD \cdot \check{\mathbf{n}})} \, d\Gamma \label{eq:Phi5C}
\end{multline}
We choose discrete spaces 
$V_{\uD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\sD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\eD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\lD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset H^1_0(\Omega)$
and $V_{\mD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset \Hdiv(\text{div};\Omega)$.
Hence, the discrete variational problem we propose for this case reads:
Find primal fields
$(\uD_h,\eD_h, \sD_h, \lD_h, \mD_h ) \in \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1} \times \RT_{k+1}
\times \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1}$, $k \ge 0$, such that
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \sD_h \cdot (\sD_h - \tilde{\sD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x} -
                \int_{\Omega}(\delta \sD_h \cdot \nabla \lD_h ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                 \int_{\Omega}(\delta \uD_h \nabla \cdot \mD_h  ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \eD_h \cdot (\eD_h - \tilde{\eD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} (\delta \eD_h \cdot \mD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
 -\int_{\Omega} \big(\sD_h \cdot \nabla \delta \lD_h + q \, \delta \lambda_h \big) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
                 \int_{\Omega}{ \delta \mD_h \cdot \eD_h}\, d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega}{\uD_h \nabla  \cdot \delta \mD_h}\, d\mathbf{x}
               - \int_{\partial{\Omega}}{ \uD_D (\delta{\mD}_h \cdot \check{\mathbf{n}})}\,d\Gamma & = 0, &
        \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$\forall (\delta\uD_h,\delta\eD_h, \delta\sD_h, \delta\lD_h, \delta\mD_h ) \in  \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1} \times \RT_{k+1}
\times \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1}$.
This discrete formulation will be termed DNLSP1-C. 

\subsubsection{Formulation DNLSP1-D}

The final variant consists in integrating by parts only the balance term in 
the original functional $\Phi_{\lD \mD}$ giving
\begin{multline}
\Phi_{\lD \mD}((\lD, \mD), (\uD, \eD, \sD); q, \Omega) = 
 \int_{\Omega}  \lD \left ( \nabla \cdot \sD - q \right ) \, d\mathbf{x} + 
\int_{\Omega} \mD \cdot \left ( \eD - \nabla {\uD} \right )\,d\mathbf{x}   \label{eq:Phi5D}
\end{multline}
We choose discrete spaces 
$V_{\uD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\sD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\eD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset L^2(\Omega)$, $V_{\lD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset H^1_0(\Omega)$
and $V_{\mD}(\mathcal{T}_h) \subset \Hdiv(\text{div};\Omega)$.
Hence, the discrete variational problem we propose for this case reads:
Find primal fields
$(\uD_h,\eD_h, \sD_h, \lD_h, \mD_h ) \in \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1} \times \RT_{k+1}
\times \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1}$, $k \ge 0$, such that
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
                \int_{\Omega}  \delta \sD_h \cdot (\sD_h - \tilde{\sD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x} +
              \int_{\Omega} {\lD_h \nabla \cdot \delta \sD_h } \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
              \int_{\Omega}(\delta \uD_h \nabla \cdot \mD_h  ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
              \int_{\Omega}  \delta \eD_h \cdot (\eD_h - \tilde{\eD}_h) \, d\mathbf{x}  + \int_{\Omega} (\delta \eD_h \cdot \mD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &\\
	      \int_{\Omega} \delta \lD_h \left ( \nabla \cdot  \sD_h  -  q \right ) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, & \\
               \int_{\Omega} \delta \mD_h \cdot (\eD_h -  \nabla \uD_h) \, d\mathbf{x} & = 0, &
        \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$\forall (\delta\uD_h,\delta\eD_h, \delta\sD_h, \delta\lD_h, \delta\mD_h ) \in  \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1} \times \RT_{k+1}
\times \DG_{k} \times \RT_{k+1}$.
This discrete formulation will be termed DNLSP1-D.

\subsection{Elasticity problem}

With reference to the general formulation we have presented in the
previous section, let us rewrite the elasticity problem with a more usual
notation in the solid mechanics context by redefining the primal
unknowns, namely, the displacement, the stress and the deformation measure
as $(u, s, e) \leftarrow (\uE, \sE, \eE)$, whereas for the dual fields, namely
the equilibrium constraint and compatibility constraint $(\lambda, \mu) \leftarrow (\lE, \mE)$.
The standard or reference formulation for this
problem in its mixed form with Dirichlet and Neumann
boundary conditions reads: Find displacement $\uE$ and 
stress $\sE$ such that
$$
\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{rcll}
\nabla \cdot \sE &=& \mathbf{f} & \mbox{in}~\Omega,\\
\\
\sE &=&  2\mu_L \,\eE + \lambda_L \nabla \cdot \eE& \mbox{in}~\Omega \\
\\
\eE &=& H(\nabla{\uE})  & \mbox{in}~\Omega \\
\\
\uE &=& \uE_0(\mathbf{x}) & \mbox{on}~\partial\Omega_{\uE} \\
\\
\sE \cdot \check{\mathbf{n}} &=& \boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}} & \mbox{on}~\partial\Omega_{\sE}
\end{array}
\right.
$$
in which the function $H$ stands for some deformation measure, e.g.,
$$
H(\eE) = \frac12 \left ( \nabla{\uE} + \nabla^{\intercal}{\uE} +  \nabla{\uE}^{\intercal} \nabla{\uE} \right )
$$ 
The model corresponds to a material with a strain-energy density 
$$
\mathcal{W} = \frac{\lambda_L}{2} \, \mbox{tr}^2(\eE) + \mu_L \,  \mbox{tr}(\eE^2)
$$
More complicated energy functions that depend on the invariants of the Cauchy strain 
tensor can be defined; however, in this article, we restrict ourselves to the linear 
elasticity case, which corresponds to the limit of infinitesimal strains. 
The nonlinear case will be addressed in future works. Moreover,
in the linear elasticity case, we have
$$
H(\eE) = \frac12 \left ( \nabla{\uE} + \nabla^{\intercal}{\uE} \right ) = \nabla^S{\uE}
$$
From the virtual work principle, the primal variational formulation for the reference problem in such 
case reads: Find $\uE \in H_0^1(\Omega)$, $\uE = \uE_0(\mathbf{x}),~~\mathbf{x} \in \partial{\Omega}_D$
such that
$$
\int_{\Omega} {\sE(\uE)} : \nabla^{S}{\mathbf{v}} \, d{\mathbf{x}} = \int_{\Omega} \mathbf{f} \cdot {\mathbf{v}}\, d{\mathbf{x}}  + \int_{\Gamma_N} {\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot \mathbf{v}}\,d\Gamma~~~\forall \mathbf{v} \in [H_0^1(\Omega)]^d
$$
